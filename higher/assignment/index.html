🧰 About the assignment

50 marks
out of 160 for the whole course — about 31% of your grade
8 hours
in class, usually over consecutive lessons once all of the course has been taught; some schools run it off-timetable over one or two days
Closed book
you only have access to the data booklet and the data sheets supplied with the assignment
Set and marked
by Qualifications Scotland, not by your teacher

The assignment gives you a series of engineering tasks in a real context, for example an off-shore wind turbine, a motor racing team or a recording studio. Each task describes the situation, what you have to do and what evidence to hand in. The tasks require you to apply your knowledge and skills from across the course: analogue and digital electronics, programmable control, pneumatics, structures and drive systems can all appear.

In the tasks you will:

  • analyse a problem, for example by drawing a control diagram from a specification;
  • design a solution, for example a logic circuit, an op-amp circuit or a flowchart;
  • build it, by simulating or constructing it;
  • test it, by planning tests, recording results and making changes;
  • evaluate it against the specification.

The rules

  • You work on your own, in class, under a high degree of supervision. You stay in your teacher’s sight, you don’t talk to other candidates, and any posters or displays that might help should be covered up.
  • It is closed book: no notes, textbooks, exemplars, internet, email or phone. You can use the Higher data booklet, the data sheets in the task, and normal classroom equipment: simulation software, pneumatics, mechanisms and electronics kits, and a PC.
  • Once you start, you carry on in each lesson until the 8 hours are used up. Your teacher gives out your work at the start of each lesson and collects it at the end. Setting up, clearing away and printing don’t count towards the 8 hours.
  • Your teacher can’t help you with the tasks, give feedback or tell you whether your work is good enough or finished. They can only help you understand the instructions, make sure you have the right equipment, and print and label your evidence. Once you hand the completed assignment in, it can’t be given back to you for more work.
  • A new assignment is set every year, and it is only valid for that session.

It must be your own work

  • You sign a declaration that the work you hand in is your own, unaided work.
  • Using AI is not allowed. Handing in anything made by a generative AI tool (text, images, code or a circuit) as your own work counts as plagiarism.
  • Copying from another candidate, working with someone else on your tasks, or having a phone, another device or handwritten notes with you during the assignment counts as malpractice, which is taken very seriously.
  • Family and friends can encourage you to prepare, but they must not tell you what to put in your assignment.
  • If you have additional support needs, ask your teacher early about assessment arrangements.

Handing in your evidence

Your work is scanned and marked from images by Qualifications Scotland markers, so it must be easy to read.

  • Write and draw in blue or black permanent ink only.
  • Put the task number at the top and your Scottish Candidate Number (SCN) at the bottom of every page.
  • Don’t glue or fix anything on to the pages, such as photographs. Print them instead.
  • Make every screenshot and photograph clear: components, connections and values must be readable.
  • Each task tells you how many pages of evidence to expect and roughly how long to spend on it. Use this to plan your 8 hours.
  • Your teacher puts the pages in task order behind a flyleaf, on A4, with one staple in the top-left corner.

Source: Qualifications Scotland Higher Engineering Science specimen assignment (2024), assignment documents 2024–2026 and Guidance on conditions of assessment for coursework (2023).

🎯 How to get the marks

The Qualifications Scotland course reports consistently say the same thing: candidates are very good at building circuits and systems, but evaluating and testing are the hardest skills. That is where most marks are lost. The advice below applies to whatever tasks your assignment sets.

Read every task carefully
  • Tasks can look very like ones in earlier assignments but ask for something quite different. Read what each task asks for, and what evidence it wants, before you start.
  • Use the correct engineering terms throughout. The reports ask for “the language and detail expected at Higher level”.
Analysing
  • Read the specification carefully and pick out every requirement: each input, output, condition and value. You will need each of these again when you test and evaluate.
  • When drawing a control or block diagram, label every sub-system and use the correct terms (input, process, output, feedback), with arrows showing the direction of the signal.
  • If a task asks for a truth table, include every combination of inputs, in order, so that no condition is missed.
Designing
  • If you are asked to produce a NAND equivalent circuit with the fewest gates, remember to simplify by removing NOT functions that are in series. Digital electronics notes
  • When comparing two logic designs for cost or manufacture, count the ICs using the data sheets you are given, not the number of gates. A 7400 IC contains four NAND gates, so four NAND gates cost one IC. Then say what fewer ICs means for cost and manufacture.
  • Show your working for any calculation in your design, such as a resistor value or an op-amp gain, so the marker can follow how you chose each value.
Building (simulating or constructing) Usually done well
  • Label any substituted components. If you use a different symbol or part because of your software or because your school doesn’t have it, label it, and make the orientation of components such as diodes, transistors and batteries clear.
  • Screenshots must be clear: components and connections easy to see, values readable and no text cut off.
  • Photographs of a pneumatic build must show the piping clearly enough to be followed. Coloured pipes help with this. The pipes must not trail out of the picture.
  • Practise with your school’s simulation software before the assignment, so you know how to make it do what a task asks.
Testing Most demanding
  • In a test plan, the test is what you do (for example “set Vin to 2.0 V”) and the expected result is what should happen (for example “Vout = −3.0 V”). Don’t mix these up.
  • Record the actual result of every test you carry out, and be specific. Writing “it worked as expected” will not get any marks; “when I pressed the switch the LED lit for 2 seconds then switched off” would be acceptable.
  • If a test fails, make the amendment before you carry out the next test, and record what you changed. Don’t move on without making the amendment the task asks for.
  • If you can’t get your system to work, say so in your test results. This can happen if you made a mistake when building your system or can’t see how to fix an error. A marker will be able to see where the fault is and will mark your results accordingly.
Evaluating Most demanding
  • Go through the specification point by point. For each one, say whether it is met and give the evidence from your own testing.
  • Refer to how your solution performed, the test results and the amendments you made.
  • Don’t claim that every point is met without evidence. Use the actual performance of your amended solution to justify each decision.
  • When a task asks whether a part can do its job, compare the numbers. For example, state the reaction force at each support and the load it can withstand, then say whether it is safe.
  • If your system doesn’t meet a specification point, say so in your evaluation. You will get a mark for this. You will lose a mark if you claim it worked when your evidence shows it didn’t.

Based on the Qualifications Scotland Higher Engineering Science course reports and advice from experienced Engineering Science teachers.

✍️ Writing an evaluation

For every point in the specification: what was required → what your solution actually did (with a value or test result) → met or not met → what you changed or would change.

This made-up example shows the level of detail expected. It is not from a real assignment.

Specification pointWeak evaluationHigher-level evaluation
The fan must switch on above 30 °C.“The fan works so this is met.”“In test 2 the fan switched on at 34 °C, so this point was not met at first. I changed R2 from 2.2 kΩ to 2.7 kΩ, and in the re-test the fan switched on at 30 °C, so this point is now met.”
The output must not exceed 10 V.“Met.”“The highest output in testing was 9.4 V, when the input was 5.0 V. This is below 10 V, so this point is met.”

The weak answers state a decision but give no evidence. The better answers quote the test results and the amendment, so the marker can see why each point is met.
