Section 1: Voltage Dividers with Sensors

Most electronic control systems start with an input sub-system that turns a physical quantity (light, temperature, position) into a voltage. At Higher this is almost always a voltage divider made from a sensor and a fixed or variable resistor. The output voltage is then passed to a transistor, a MOSFET or an op-amp.

Vout = R2R1 + R2 × VS

where R2 is the resistor across which Vout is measured (normally the lower one). You can also use the fact that the supply voltage divides between the two resistors in the same ratio as their resistances: V1V2 = R1R2.

Which way round?

SensorResistance when…In the lower position, Vout…In the upper position, Vout…
LDRhigh in the dark, low in bright lightrises as it gets darker (dark sensor)rises as it gets lighter (light sensor)
NTC thermistorhigh when cold, low when hotrises as it gets colder (cold sensor)rises as it gets hotter (heat sensor)
Variable resistorset by handsets a reference voltage or adjusts the switching point
Rule of thumb: Vout follows the resistance of whichever component it is measured across. If the sensor is at the bottom, Vout rises when the sensor’s resistance rises.

Sensor characteristics

Manufacturers publish how a sensor’s resistance changes, either as a graph (often on a logarithmic scale, because the resistance changes over a very wide range) or as a table. The values below are typical and are used throughout these notes.

Section 2: Designing Sensor Voltage Dividers

At Higher you must be able to design a voltage divider to meet a specification. Typically you are told the supply voltage, the condition at which something should happen (for example 30 °C), and the voltage Vout must reach at that point. You read the sensor’s resistance at that condition from its characteristics, then calculate the other resistor.

Worked example — heat sensor for a 9 V system
1
Specification: thermistor at the top, 9 V supply, Vout must be 6.0 V at 30 °C.
2
From the table, the thermistor is 4.0 kΩ at 30 °C. The fixed resistor R2 (bottom) must have 6.0 V across it, so the thermistor has 9 − 6 = 3.0 V across it.
3
Ratio: R2Rth = 6.03.0, so R2 = 4.0 × 2 = 8.0 kΩ.
4
Check at 40 °C (2.6 kΩ): Vout = 8.0 / (2.6 + 8.0) × 9 = 6.8 V. Vout rises when hotter — correct for a heat sensor.
Real resistor values. Fixed resistors only come in preferred values (for example 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.7, 3.3, 3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8, 8.2 × a power of ten). If your calculated value is not available, choose the nearest one and work out the effect — or use a variable resistor so the switching point can be adjusted on site.

Section 3: The BJT as a Current Amplifier

At National 5 you used the bipolar junction transistor (BJT) as a switch. At Higher you also need to treat it as a current amplifier: a small current into the base (IB) controls a much larger current through the collector and emitter (IC). The ratio of the two currents is the transistor’s current gain, hFE.

hFE = ICIB      so      IC = hFE × IB
SymbolMeaning
IBbase current
ICcollector current
IEemitter current: IE = IB + IC (and since IB is tiny, IE ≈ IC)
VBEbase–emitter voltage: about 0.7 V when the transistor is conducting
VCEcollector–emitter voltage
VLvoltage across the load: VCC = VL + VCE
The base resistor RB limits the base current. It has (Vin − 0.7) V across it, so IB = (Vin − 0.7) / RB.
Worked example — analysing a BJT circuit
1
A transistor with hFE = 200 has RB = 150 kΩ connected to 9 V, and a 470 Ω load on a 9 V supply. Calculate IC and VCE.
2
Voltage across RB = 9 − 0.7 = 8.3 V, so IB = 8.3 / 150 000 = 55.3 µA.
3
IC = 200 × 55.3 µA = 11.1 mA.
4
VL = 0.0111 × 470 = 5.20 V, so VCE = 9 − 5.20 = 3.80 V.
Worked example — designing a BJT driver
1
A 9 V relay coil needs 75 mA. The transistor has hFE = 150 and is switched by a 5 V signal. Choose RB.
2
Base current needed: IB = IC / hFE = 0.075 / 150 = 0.5 mA.
3
RB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.0005 = 8.6 kΩ.
4
Choose the next lower preferred value, 8.2 kΩ: a slightly larger base current makes sure the transistor is fully on.
Protect the transistor. Relays, motors and solenoids are inductive: when they are switched off they produce a large reverse voltage (back emf). A diode connected in reverse across the coil gives this a safe path and protects the transistor.

Section 4: The MOSFET as a Voltage-Operated Switch

A MOSFET (metal oxide semiconductor field effect transistor) is another type of transistor. At Higher you need the n-channel enhancement mode MOSFET. Its three terminals are the gate (g), drain (d) and source (s) — they do the same jobs as the base, collector and emitter of an npn BJT.

The gate is insulated from the rest of the device, so no current flows into the gate. The MOSFET is controlled by the voltage between gate and source, VGS. Below the threshold voltage VT it is off; once VGS reaches VT it switches on and a drain current ID flows through the load.

MOSFET on when VGS ≥ VT

Because the gate draws no current, the voltage divider that drives it is not affected by the MOSFET. That means the voltage divider rule gives the gate voltage exactly. To design the circuit, choose the resistor so that VGS = VT at the condition where the load should switch on.

Worked example — designing a MOSFET fan switch
1
The circuit above runs from 12 V. The MOSFET has VT = 3.0 V. The fan must switch on at 30 °C, when the thermistor is 4.0 kΩ. Find RV.
2
RV needs 3.0 V across it and the thermistor 12 − 3.0 = 9.0 V.
3
RV = 4.0 × 3.0 / 9.0 = 1.33 kΩ.
4
Above 30 °C the thermistor’s resistance falls, VGS rises above 3.0 V, and the fan stays on.
When the MOSFET is fully on, the voltage between drain and source is very small, so almost all of the supply is across the load: ID ≈ Vsupply / Rload.

Section 6: The Operational Amplifier

An operational amplifier (op-amp) is an integrated circuit that amplifies voltages. It has two inputs — the inverting input (−) and the non-inverting input (+) — and one output. On its own its gain is enormous, so it is almost always used with resistors that set the gain to a sensible value.

Voltage gain   AV = VoVin

Saturation

The output can never go beyond the supply voltage. In Higher Engineering Science we assume the output can reach about 85% of the supply voltage. If the calculated output is bigger than this, the op-amp is saturated and the output stays at the limit.

With a ±12 V supply, the output can only reach ±(0.85 × 12) = ±10.2 V. Always check your answer against this limit.

An ideal op-amp also has a very high input resistance (it draws almost no current from whatever is connected to its inputs) and a very low output resistance (it can drive a load without its output voltage dropping).

Section 7: The Inverting Amplifier

The input signal goes to the inverting input through R1. The feedback resistor Rf connects the output back to the inverting input, and the non-inverting input is connected to 0 V.

AV = −RfR1      Vo = −RfR1 × Vin
The minus sign means the output is inverted: a positive input gives a negative output, and vice versa.
Worked example
1
R1 = 15 kΩ, Rf = 470 kΩ, Vin = +0.2 V, supply ±12 V.
2
AV = −470 / 15 = −31.3
3
Vo = −31.3 × 0.2 = −6.27 V — within the ±10.2 V limit, so not saturated.

Section 8: The Non-Inverting Amplifier

The input goes straight to the non-inverting input. Rf and R1 feed a fraction of the output back to the inverting input. The output has the same polarity as the input.

AV = 1 + RfR1      Vo = (1 + RfR1) × Vin
The gain is always at least 1. Because the signal goes straight into an op-amp input, this circuit has a very high input resistance — ideal for amplifying a sensor that cannot supply much current.

Section 9: The Voltage Follower

The voltage follower is a non-inverting amplifier with the output connected directly to the inverting input. Its gain is exactly 1, so Vo = Vin.

Vo = Vin    (AV = 1)

If it does not amplify, why use it? Because it has a very high input resistance and a very low output resistance. It takes almost no current from a sensor voltage divider, but can supply the current a following stage or meter needs without its voltage dropping. It is used as a buffer between stages of a circuit.

Section 10: The Comparator

A comparator uses the op-amp with no feedback resistor (open loop). Its huge gain means even a tiny difference between the inputs drives the output to saturation. It simply compares the two input voltages:

V+ > V− → Vo = +Vsat      V+ < V− → Vo = −Vsat

One input is a reference voltage, set by a fixed voltage divider or potentiometer. The other is the sensor voltage from an LDR or thermistor divider. The switching point is where the sensor voltage equals the reference.

Two-state control. The output is either fully high or fully low — like an on/off thermostat. This is ideal for alarms, frost warnings and lights that just need switching on or off. The output usually drives a transistor or MOSFET, which switches the actual load.

To make the output go high for the condition you want, connect the sensor to the input that makes V+ > V− under that condition. In the circuit above, the sensor voltage V1 rises as it gets darker. In the dark, V1 rises above the reference V2, so V+ < V− and the output goes low. To make it go high in the dark instead, swap the two inputs, or swap the LDR and R1.

Section 11: The Difference Amplifier

Like the comparator, the difference amplifier has a signal on each input. Unlike the comparator, it has feedback, so its output is proportional to the difference between the two inputs — not simply fully on or off.

Vo = RfR1 × (V2 − V1)

Here V2 is on the non-inverting side and V1 on the inverting side. The two resistors called R1 must be equal, and so must the two called Rf.

Proportional control and error detection. If V1 is a set point (what we want) and V2 comes from a sensor (what we have), V2 − V1 is the error. A large error gives a large output; as the system approaches the set point the output falls, and at the set point it is zero. This is how a closed-loop system can respond gradually instead of switching on and off.
Worked example — incubator heater
1
A temperature sensor gives V2 = 3.4 V; the set point is V1 = 3.0 V; R1 = 10 kΩ and Rf = 100 kΩ.
2
Vo = (100/10) × (3.4 − 3.0) = 4.0 V.
3
As the error shrinks, Vo falls in proportion — at an error of 0.1 V, Vo is only 1.0 V.

Section 12: The Summing Amplifier

A summing amplifier is an inverting amplifier with two or more inputs, each connected through its own resistor to the inverting input. The output is the inverted sum of the inputs, each multiplied by its own gain.

Vo = −Rf × (V1R1 + V2R2 + V3R3)
The output is inverted. To get a positive result, feed the output through a second inverting amplifier with a gain of −1 (R1 = Rf).
Worked example
1
V1 = 4 V (R1 = 10 kΩ), V2 = 2 V (R2 = 5 kΩ), V3 = 1 V (R3 = 2 kΩ), Rf = 5 kΩ, supply ±15 V.
2
Vo = −5000 × (4/10 000 + 2/5000 + 1/2000)
3
= −5000 × 0.0013 = −6.5 V (inside the ±12.75 V limit).

Section 14: Design Challenges

Each challenge gives you a real context and a written specification, like an engineering brief. You will analyse the problem, design a circuit, build or simulate it, test it and evaluate it — the same stages as the Higher assignment.

How to approach these: read the whole specification first. Identify the input, process and output sub-systems. Do your calculations before you build. In your evaluation, refer back to each numbered point in the specification and say whether, and how well, it is met.
