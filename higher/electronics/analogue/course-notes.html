1 — Voltage Dividers with Input Transducers

At N5 you used a fixed resistor and an LDR or thermistor in a voltage divider to produce a voltage that changed with a physical quantity. At Higher you need to be able to design the divider to meet a specification — choosing the correct resistor value to produce a required output voltage.

The three input transducers you need to know for Higher are:

Input Transducers Summary
Transducer Measures Resistance changes
LDR (Light Dependent Resistor)Light level (lux)High in dark → low in bright light
NTC ThermistorTemperatureHigh when cold → low when hot
Variable Resistor (potentiometer)Position / angleSet manually — used as a reference

At Higher you will be given characteristic graphs or tables for LDRs and thermistors. You must read the resistance at the relevant value, then substitute it into the voltage divider formula to find Vout, or rearrange to find the required fixed resistor.

Vout = (R2 / (R1 + R2)) × VS    or    V1 / V2 = R1 / R2
Design tip: Voltage is the same across components connected in parallel. Any output device or amplifier connected across R2 sees the same voltage as R2.
Worked Example — Designing a Sensor Voltage Divider

A thermistor has a resistance of 4 kΩ at 20 °C. Design a voltage divider connected to a 9 V supply so that Vout = 3 V at this temperature. The thermistor is in the lower (R2) position.

Step 1 — Apply the voltage divider rule:

Vout = (R2 / (R1 + R2)) × VS

3 = (4000 / (R1 + 4000)) × 9

Step 2 — Rearrange:

R1 + 4000 = (4000 × 9) / 3 = 12 000

R1 = 12 000 − 4000 = 8 kΩ

2 — The BJT as a Current Amplifier

At National 5 you used the transistor as a simple electronic switch: when VBE ≥ 0.7 V the transistor turned ON, connecting the output device to the supply. At Higher, you need to understand that the transistor also acts as a current amplifier.

A small current flowing into the base (IB) allows a much larger current to flow between the collector and emitter (IC). The ratio of these currents is the current gain, given the symbol hFE:

hFE = IC / IB     therefore     IC = hFE × IB

Transistor Notation

Key Variables
ICCollector current
IBBase current
IEEmitter current  (IE = IB + IC ≈ IC)
VCCSupply voltage (relative to 0 V)
VBEBase–emitter voltage  (0.7 V when saturated)
VCECollector–emitter voltage
VLVoltage across the load resistor
hFECurrent gain of the transistor
Key relationship: VCC = VL + VCE    (with no emitter resistor). Since IB is so small compared to IC, it is standard practice to assume IE ≈ IC in calculations.

Designing a BJT Circuit

A typical BJT driver circuit has a base resistor (RB) connected between the input/voltage divider and the base. This protects the transistor from excessive base current. The load (e.g. LED, motor, relay) is in the collector line.

Worked Example — BJT Current Amplifier

A transistor (hFE = 200) has a base resistor RB = 150 kΩ connected directly to a 9 V supply. A load resistor RL = 470 Ω is in the collector line. Calculate the collector current and VCE.

Step 1 — Voltage across RB:

VRB = VCC − VBE = 9 − 0.7 = 8.3 V

Step 2 — Base current:

IB = VRB / RB = 8.3 / 150 000 = 5.53 × 10−5 A

Step 3 — Collector current:

IC = hFE × IB = 200 × 5.53 × 10−5 = 0.011 A

Step 4 — Voltage across load:

VL = IC × RL = 0.011 × 470 = 5.17 V

Step 5 — VCE:

VCE = VCC − VL = 9 − 5.17 = 3.83 V

3 — The MOSFET as a Voltage-Operated Switch

A Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor (MOSFET) is an alternative to the BJT for switching applications. At Higher you only need the n-channel enhancement mode MOSFET.

How the MOSFET Works

The MOSFET has three terminals: Gate (G), Drain (D) and Source (S) — analogous to base, collector and emitter of a BJT. The key difference is that the MOSFET is voltage-operated, not current-operated. No current flows into the gate.

When the gate-source voltage (VGS) is below the threshold voltage (VT), no current flows between drain and source — the device is OFF. When VGS exceeds VT, the MOSFET switches ON and drain current (ID) flows. The higher VGS goes above VT, the more strongly the device conducts.

MOSFET switches ON when: VGS ≥ VT (threshold voltage)

MOSFET Circuit Calculations

Because no current enters the gate, Ohm's Law applies directly to the drain-source-load part of the circuit. When the MOSFET is fully ON (saturated):

VCC = VDS + Vload     and     ID = Vload / Rload
Worked Example — MOSFET Switching Circuit

A MOSFET with a threshold voltage of 2 V has a 100 Ω load connected to a 5 V supply. A voltage divider is used to set VGS = 3.5 V. Is the MOSFET ON? If so, calculate the drain current.

VGS = 3.5 V > VT = 2 V → MOSFET is ON

ID = VCC / Rload = 5 / 100 = 50 mA  (assuming VDS ≈ 0 V when fully saturated)

BJT vs MOSFET — Comparison

You must be able to compare the two devices and justify which is more appropriate for a given application.

BJT vs MOSFET Comparison Table
Feature BJT MOSFET
Operated byCurrent (IB)Voltage (VGS)
Input current neededYes (small IB)No (gate is insulated)
Suitable for high currentsModerateYes — very high ID possible
Switching speedFastCan be slower (gate capacitance)

Exam tip: A MOSFET is preferred when: (a) the driver is a microcontroller (voltage output only), (b) very high drain currents are needed, or (c) power efficiency is important (very low gate power). A BJT is preferred when fast switching is critical.

4 — The Operational Amplifier (Op-Amp)

An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a high-gain voltage amplifier. It amplifies the difference between its two input voltages. The op-amp symbol shows two inputs: the inverting input (−) and the non-inverting input (+).

Ideal Op-Amp Properties
  • Infinite input resistance — draws no current from the source
  • Zero output resistance — can supply current without voltage drop
  • Very high open-loop gain (A0 ≈ 100 000)
  • Output limited to approximately 85% of supply voltage (saturation)

The basic voltage gain formula applies to all configurations:

AV = Vout / Vin

You need to know six configurations at Higher. Each is covered in the sections below.

5 — Inverting Amplifier

The signal is connected to the inverting (−) input through an input resistor R1. A feedback resistor Rf connects the output back to the inverting input. The non-inverting input is connected to 0 V.

AV = −Rf / R1      Vout = −(Rf / R1) × Vin
The negative sign means the output is inverted — if the input is positive, the output is negative, and vice versa.
Worked Example — Inverting Amplifier

R1 = 15 kΩ, Rf = 470 kΩ, Vin = +0.2 V. Find the gain and Vout.

AV = −Rf / R1 = −470 / 15 = −31.3

Vout = −31.3 × 0.2 = −6.27 V

6 — Non-Inverting Amplifier

The signal is connected directly to the non-inverting (+) input. Rf and R1 form a voltage divider from the output back to the inverting (−) input. The output is always the same polarity as the input.

AV = 1 + Rf / R1      Vout = (1 + Rf / R1) × Vin
Key point: The gain is always greater than 1 for the non-inverting configuration. Because the input resistance is very high (ideally infinite), this configuration is useful when the input transducer cannot supply large currents.

7 — Voltage Follower

The voltage follower is a special case of the non-inverting amplifier where the output is connected directly to the inverting input (Rf = 0, R1 = ∞). Since gain = 1 + 0/∞ = 1, Vout = Vin.

Vout = Vin     (gain = 1)

Its purpose is impedance matching: the circuit has a very high input resistance (so it draws almost no current from the source) and a very low output resistance (so it can drive a low-resistance load). It is often used to isolate or “buffer” one stage of a circuit from another.

8 — Comparator

The comparator uses the op-amp in open-loop mode — there is no feedback resistor. The open-loop gain is enormous (≈100 000), so even the tiniest difference between the two inputs drives the output into positive or negative saturation.

If V+ > V− → Vout = +Vsat (≈ +0.85 × VCC)
If V+ < V− → Vout = −Vsat (≈ −0.85 × VCC)
Two-state control: The output is either fully ON or fully OFF — there is no “in between”. This makes the comparator ideal for simple switching applications such as turning a heater on when temperature drops below a set point.

In a typical circuit, one input is a reference voltage (set by a fixed voltage divider or potentiometer), and the other is the sensor voltage from an LDR or thermistor divider.

9 — Difference Amplifier

The difference amplifier uses closed-loop feedback. Like the comparator, it has two inputs, but unlike the comparator the output is proportional to the difference between those inputs — not just fully on or off.

Each input has its own resistor, and Rf is the feedback resistor. When all four resistors are equal:

Vout = (Rf / R1) × (V2 − V1)
Proportional control: The output is proportional to the error (difference between desired and actual values). Large error = large output; zero error = zero output. This is used in high-precision control systems such as medical equipment and process control.
Worked Example — Difference Amplifier

Two strain gauges form the inputs of a difference amplifier. When unstrained, both have resistance 200 Ω and V1 = V2 = 3 V. When Rg2 increases to 210 Ω, V2 rises to 3.05 V. All four op-amp resistors are 42 kΩ. Calculate Vout.

AV = Rf / R1 = 42 k / 42 k = 1

Vout = 1 × (3.05 − 3.00) = 0.05 V

In a real instrument the gain would be much higher (e.g. 10 000) to give a readable output.

10 — Summing Amplifier

The summing amplifier is a variation of the inverting amplifier with two or more inputs, each connected through its own resistor to the inverting terminal. The op-amp amplifies each input independently and then sums the results.

Vout = −Rf × (V1/R1 + V2/R2 + V3/R3 + …)

When all input resistors are equal (R1 = R2 = R3 = R):

Vout = −(Rf / R) × (V1 + V2 + V3 + …)
Remember the output is inverted (negative sign). To get a positive sum, the output is often passed through a second inverting amplifier with gain −1 (R1 = Rf).
Worked Example — Summing Amplifier

Three inputs: V1 = 4 V (R1 = 10 kΩ), V2 = 2 V (R2 = 5 kΩ), V3 = 1 V (R3 = 2 kΩ). Rf = 10 kΩ. Find Vout.

Vout = −10 000 × (4/10 000 + 2/5000 + 1/2000)

= −10 000 × (0.0004 + 0.0004 + 0.0005) = −10 000 × 0.0013 = −13 V
