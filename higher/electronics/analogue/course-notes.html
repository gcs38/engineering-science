Before You Start

This topic builds directly on your National 5 work. Before continuing, make sure you are confident with:

  • The difference between analogue and digital signals

1 — Voltage Dividers with Input Transducers

At N5 you used a fixed resistor and an LDR or thermistor in a voltage divider to produce a voltage that changed with a physical quantity. At Higher you need to be able to design the divider to meet a specification — choosing the correct resistor value to produce a required output voltage.

TransducerMeasuresResistance changes
LDR (Light Dependent Resistor)Light level (lux)High in dark → low in bright light
NTC ThermistorTemperatureHigh when cold → low when hot
Variable Resistor (potentiometer)Position / angleSet manually — used as a reference
Vout = (R2 / (R1 + R2)) × VS
📷 Diagram: voltage divider with LDR and fixed resistor
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-voltage-divider-sensor.png
Worked Example — Designing a Sensor Voltage Divider

A thermistor has a resistance of 4 kΩ at 20 °C. Design a voltage divider connected to a 9 V supply so that Vout = 3 V. The thermistor is in the lower (R2) position.

Step 1 — Apply the voltage divider rule:

3 = (4000 / (R1 + 4000)) × 9

Step 2 — Rearrange:

R1 + 4000 = (4000 × 9) / 3 = 12 000

R1 = 12 000 − 4000 = 8 kΩ

2 — The BJT as a Current Amplifier

At National 5 you used the transistor as a simple electronic switch. At Higher you need to understand that it also acts as a current amplifier. A small current into the base (IB) allows a much larger current to flow between the collector and emitter (IC). The ratio of these currents is the current gain hFE:

hFE = IC / IB     therefore     IC = hFE × IB

Transistor Notation

SymbolMeaning
ICCollector current
IBBase current
IEEmitter current  (IE = IB + IC ≈ IC)
VCCSupply voltage (relative to 0 V)
VBEBase–emitter voltage  (0.7 V when saturated)
VCECollector–emitter voltage
VLVoltage across the load resistor
hFECurrent gain of the transistor
VCC = VL + VCE    (with no emitter resistor). Since IB is so small, assume IE ≈ IC in calculations.
📷 Diagram: BJT circuit showing collector, base and emitter currents
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-bjt-circuit.png
Worked Example — BJT Current Amplifier

A transistor (hFE = 200) has RB = 150 kΩ connected to a 9 V supply. RL = 470 Ω. Calculate IC and VCE.

Step 1 — Voltage across RB:   VRB = 9 − 0.7 = 8.3 V

Step 2 — Base current:   IB = 8.3 / 150 000 = 5.53 × 10−5 A

Step 3 — Collector current:   IC = 200 × 5.53 × 10−5 = 0.011 A

Step 4 — Voltage across load:   VL = 0.011 × 470 = 5.17 V

Step 5 — VCE = 9 − 5.17 = 3.83 V

3 — The MOSFET as a Voltage-Operated Switch

A Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor (MOSFET) is an alternative to the BJT. At Higher you only need the n-channel enhancement mode MOSFET. It has three terminals: Gate (G), Drain (D) and Source (S) — analogous to base, collector and emitter of a BJT.

The key difference is that the MOSFET is voltage-operated — no current flows into the gate. When the gate-source voltage (VGS) is below the threshold voltage (VT), the device is OFF. When VGS ≥ VT, the MOSFET switches ON and drain current (ID) flows.

MOSFET switches ON when: VGS ≥ VT
📷 Diagram: n-channel enhancement MOSFET symbol and switching circuit
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-mosfet-circuit.png
Because no current enters the gate, Ohm's Law applies directly to the drain-source circuit: VCC = VDS + Vload    and    ID = Vload / Rload

BJT vs MOSFET

FeatureBJTMOSFET
Operated byCurrent (IB)Voltage (VGS)
Input current neededYes (small IB)No (gate is insulated)
High current capabilityModerateYes — very high ID possible
Drive from microcontrollerNeeds base currentIdeal — voltage output only
Static damage riskLowHigh — handle with care

4 — The Operational Amplifier

An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a high-gain voltage amplifier that amplifies the difference between its two input voltages. It has an inverting input (−) and a non-inverting input (+). You need to know six configurations at Higher.

AV = Vout / Vin
The output is limited to approximately 85% of the supply voltage. If VCC = ±12 V, the maximum output is ±10.2 V. Beyond this the op-amp is saturated.
📷 Diagram: op-amp symbol showing −, + inputs, output and dual supply
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-opamp-symbol.png

5 — Inverting Amplifier

The signal connects to the inverting (−) input through R1. A feedback resistor Rf connects the output back to the inverting input. The non-inverting input is connected to 0 V.

📷 Diagram: inverting amplifier with R1 and Rf labelled
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-inverting-amp.png
AV = −Rf / R1      Vout = −(Rf / R1) × Vin
The negative sign means the output is inverted — a positive input gives a negative output.
Worked Example

R1 = 15 kΩ, Rf = 470 kΩ, Vin = +0.2 V.

AV = −470 / 15 = −31.3

Vout = −31.3 × 0.2 = −6.27 V

6 — Non-Inverting Amplifier

The signal connects directly to the non-inverting (+) input. Rf and R1 feed back some of the output to the inverting input. The output is the same polarity as the input.

📷 Diagram: non-inverting amplifier circuit
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-noninverting-amp.png
AV = 1 + Rf / R1      Vout = (1 + Rf / R1) × Vin
Gain is always greater than 1. The very high input resistance makes this useful when the input transducer cannot supply large currents.

7 — Voltage Follower

A special case of the non-inverting amplifier where the output is connected directly to the inverting input (Rf = 0). Gain = 1, so Vout = Vin. Its purpose is impedance matching: very high input resistance, very low output resistance. Used to buffer or isolate circuit stages from each other.

📷 Diagram: voltage follower — output connected directly to inverting input
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-voltage-follower.png
Vout = Vin     (gain = 1)

8 — Comparator

The comparator uses the op-amp in open-loop mode — no feedback resistor. The enormous open-loop gain drives the output to full positive or negative saturation based on which input is higher.

V+ > V− → Vout = +Vsat     V+ < V− → Vout = −Vsat
Two-state control: the output is either fully ON or fully OFF. Ideal for simple switching applications such as thermostats and alarms.

One input is a reference voltage (fixed divider or potentiometer); the other is the sensor voltage from an LDR or thermistor divider.

9 — Difference Amplifier

Like the comparator, the difference amplifier has two inputs. Unlike the comparator, it uses closed-loop feedback so the output is proportional to the difference between the inputs — not simply fully on or off.

📷 Diagram: difference amplifier with four equal resistors labelled R1 and Rf
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-difference-amp.png
Vout = (Rf / R1) × (V2 − V1)
Proportional control: large error = large output; zero error = zero output. Used in high-precision systems such as medical equipment, strain gauge amplifiers and process control.

10 — Summing Amplifier

A variation of the inverting amplifier with two or more inputs, each connected through its own resistor to the inverting terminal. The op-amp amplifies each input independently and sums the results.

📷 Diagram: summing amplifier with three inputs V1, V2, V3 through R1, R2, R3 and feedback Rf
Image file: higher/electronics/analogue/images/ae-summing-amp.png
Vout = −Rf × (V1/R1 + V2/R2 + V3/R3)
The output is inverted. To obtain a positive result, pass the output through a second inverting amplifier with gain −1 (R1 = Rf).
Worked Example

V1 = 4 V (R1 = 10 kΩ), V2 = 2 V (R2 = 5 kΩ), V3 = 1 V (R3 = 2 kΩ), Rf = 10 kΩ.

Vout = −10 000 × (4/10 000 + 2/5000 + 1/2000) = −10 000 × 0.0013 = −13 V
