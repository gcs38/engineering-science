Section 1: Structures and Loads

A structure is anything designed to carry a load and transfer it safely to the ground (or to another structure). Engineers must be able to calculate the forces inside a structure before it is built, so that every part can be made strong enough — and no stronger than it needs to be, because extra material costs money and adds weight.

Types of structure

TypeHow it carries loadScottish examples
MassResists loads by its own weight. Usually solid and very heavy.Concrete dams such as Cruachan; stone harbour walls; the piers of the Forth Bridge
FrameAn arrangement of members (bars) joined at nodes. Triangulated so that each member is only pushed or pulled. A flat frame is a plane frame; a three-dimensional one is a space frame.The Forth Bridge, electricity pylons, roof trusses, the Falkirk Wheel’s steel arms, festival stage rigs
ShellA thin, curved or folded skin carries the load. Strength comes from the shape of the skin.Car and racing-car monocoques, aircraft fuselages, the hull of a ferry, a canoe

At Higher you will mainly analyse frame structures and beams. A beam is a member that carries loads across a gap, such as a walkway or a bridge deck.

Struts and ties

In a frame, every member is either being squashed or stretched:

  • A strut is a member in compression. The loads try to shorten it, so it pushes outwards on the nodes at each end.
  • A tie is a member in tension. The loads try to stretch it, so it pulls inwards on the nodes at each end.
At a node: draw a tie as an arrow pointing away from the node (it pulls the node along the member) and a strut as an arrow pointing towards the node (it pushes the node). You will use this in every nodal analysis question.

Loads and free-body diagrams

A static load does not change (the weight of a bridge deck); a dynamic load changes or moves (traffic, wind, a crowd jumping at a concert). Loads are shown as forces in newtons (N), kilonewtons (kN, × 103) or meganewtons (MN, × 106). If you are given a mass, its weight is W = mg, with g = 9.8 N kg−1.

A free-body diagram shows one structure (or one part of it, such as a single node) with every force acting on it — loads and reactions — drawn as arrows, with the angles and distances needed for calculations. Drawing a clear free-body diagram is always the first step.

Section 2: Resolving Forces

Scalar quantities have size only (mass, volume, energy). Vector quantities have size and direction (force, velocity, displacement). A force is drawn as an arrow: its length shows the magnitude and its angle shows the direction.

A force acting at an angle has two effects at once: it pushes or pulls sideways and up or down. Resolving a force means replacing it with two forces at right angles — a horizontal component FH and a vertical component FV — that together have exactly the same effect.

FH = F cos θ      FV = F sin θ

where θ is the angle between the force and the horizontal. These come straight from SOH CAH TOA: the force is the hypotenuse, FH is adjacent to θ and FV is opposite.

Worked example — resolving the 90 N force
1
FH = 90 cos 30° = 77.94… N, acting to the left (the force acts down and to the left in the beam diagram below).
2
FV = 90 sin 30° = 45 N, acting downwards.
3
Answers: FH = 78 N and FV = 45 N. Check: √(77.942 + 452) = 90 N. ✓
Check which angle you are given. If the angle is measured from the vertical, the components swap: FH = F sin θ and FV = F cos θ. Always ask yourself: is the component adjacent to the angle, or opposite it? Exam diagrams use both.

From components back to a single force

If you know both components, Pythagoras gives the size of the force and tan gives its angle to the horizontal:

F = √(FH2 + FV2)      θ = tan−1(FV / FH)

Always state the direction in words as well as the angle, for example “38° above the horizontal, to the left”, or mark it on a sketch.

Section 3: Resultant and Equilibrant

When several forces act through the same point they form a concurrent force system. Their combined effect is the same as a single force called the resultant.

  • The resultant is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces together.
  • The equilibrant is the single force that would balance them, holding the point in equilibrium. It is the same size as the resultant but acts in exactly the opposite direction.

Exam questions usually say “calculate the magnitude and angle of the force required to maintain equilibrium” — that is the equilibrant.

Method: components

  1. Resolve every known force into horizontal and vertical components. Take right and up as positive.
  2. Add the horizontal components to get ΣFH, and the vertical components to get ΣFV.
  3. The resultant has these components. The equilibrant has the same size with both directions reversed.
  4. Use Pythagoras for the magnitude and tan−1 for the angle. Sketch the components to see which quadrant the force points into.
Worked example — force needed for equilibrium
1
Horizontal components: 600 cos 50° = 385.7 N (right) and 250 cos 35° = 204.8 N (right).
ΣFH = 385.7 + 204.8 = 590.5 N to the right.
2
Vertical components: 600 sin 50° = 459.6 N (up) and 250 sin 35° = 143.4 N (down).
ΣFV = 459.6 − 143.4 = 316.2 N upwards.
3
For equilibrium, R must supply 590.5 N to the left and 316.2 N downwards.
4
R = √(590.52 + 316.22) = 669.8… N   →  R = 670 N
5
θ = tan−1(316.2 / 590.5) = 28° below the horizontal, to the left.

Triangle and polygon of forces

Vectors can also be added by drawing them tip to tail: draw the first force to scale, start the next one where the first ends, and so on. The resultant is the arrow from the start of the first force to the end of the last. This is a triangle of forces (two forces) or a polygon of forces (three or more).

If the forces are in equilibrium, the triangle or polygon closes — the last arrow ends exactly where the first began, because the resultant is zero. This is why an accurate scale drawing can be used to find unknown forces, as you did at National 5.

Because the forces form a triangle, you can also solve it with the cosine rule and the sine rule from Higher Maths instead of a scale drawing:

a2 = b2 + c2 − 2bc cos A      a / sin A = b / sin B
Worked example — triangle of forces
1
Two forces of 6.0 kN and 4.0 kN act at a point, 50° apart. Draw them tip to tail: the angle between them inside the triangle is 180° − 50° = 130°.
2
Cosine rule: R2 = 6.02 + 4.02 − 2 × 6.0 × 4.0 × cos 130° = 82.85, so R = 9.1 kN.
3
Sine rule for the angle α between R and the 6.0 kN force: sin α = 4.0 sin 130° / 9.102, so α = 20°.
4
The equilibrant is 9.1 kN acting in the opposite direction to R.

Either method earns full marks. The component method works for any number of forces, so it is the one to use for most exam questions.

Section 4: Equilibrium of Concurrent Forces

A point (or a node, or a whole structure) is in static equilibrium when it is at rest and stays at rest. For forces that all act through one point, this means:

ΣFH = 0      ΣFV = 0

In words: the forces to the left balance the forces to the right, and the forces up balance the forces down. Each equation lets you find one unknown, so at a single point you can find at most two unknown forces.

Two unknown forces: simultaneous equations

Often the directions of two forces are known (they act along cables or members) but not their sizes. Writing ΣFH = 0 and ΣFV = 0 then gives two equations with two unknowns. Rearrange one equation to make one unknown the subject, then substitute it into the other. This is the “substitution in simultaneous equations” skill named in the course specification.

Worked example — tension in two cables
1
Forces on the joint: T1 at 30° above the horizontal to the left, T2 at 45° above the horizontal to the right, and 25 kN downwards.
2
ΣFH = 0:   T1 cos 30° = T2 cos 45°  →  T2 = T1 cos 30° / cos 45° = 1.2247 T1  (equation 1)
3
ΣFV = 0:   T1 sin 30° + T2 sin 45° = 25  (equation 2)
4
Substitute equation 1 into equation 2: T1 sin 30° + 1.2247 T1 sin 45° = 25  →  1.3660 T1 = 25
5
T1 = 18.30… kN = 18 kN;   T2 = 1.2247 × 18.30 = 22.41… kN = 22 kN
6
Check with a triangle of forces: the angles in the triangle are 45°, 60° and 75°, so by the sine rule T1 = 25 sin 45° / sin 75° = 18.30 kN. ✓
Keep the unrounded multiplier. Rounding T2 = 1.2247T1 to 1.2T1 part-way through can change your final answer. Store values in your calculator’s memory.

Writing the equations in exam questions

Some questions ask you only to write the equation for the vertical or horizontal forces on a node, “in its simplest form”. Put the forces acting one way on one side and the forces acting the other way on the other side, each written as force × sin or cos of its angle. For example:  T1 sin 30° + T2 sin 45° = 25.

Section 5: Moments of Angled Forces

A force that does not pass through a point has a turning effect, or moment, about that point:

Moment = force × perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force

Moments are measured in N m or kN m. A body in equilibrium does not turn, so the principle of moments applies about any point: the sum of the clockwise moments equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments, ΣM = 0. This is the third condition of static equilibrium.

Forces at an angle

When a force acts at an angle to a beam, the distance along the beam is not the perpendicular distance. There are two equivalent ways to deal with this:

  • Resolve the force into a component perpendicular to the beam and a component along the beam. The component along the beam passes through the pivot, so it has no moment. Only the perpendicular component turns the beam.
  • Find the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action using trigonometry, and multiply by the whole force.
Worked example — force at an angle
1
A 2.4 m bar is hinged at O and held horizontal by a force F at its end, acting at 60° to the bar. A 150 N load acts 1.0 m from O. Find F.
2
Only the perpendicular component of F, F sin 60°, has a moment about O. Its component along the bar, F cos 60°, passes through O.
3
ΣCWM = ΣACWM:   150 × 1.0 = F sin 60° × 2.4
4
F = 150 / (sin 60° × 2.4) = 72.17… N = 72 N

Section 6: Beam Reactions

A simply supported beam rests on two supports. The forces the supports push back with are the reactions. Because the beam is in equilibrium, all three conditions apply: ΣM = 0, ΣFV = 0 and ΣFH = 0.

Types of support

SupportReaction it can provideUnknowns
Simple (knife edge)Perpendicular to the surface it rests on (vertical, for a horizontal beam). Used when there are no sideways loads.1
RollerOnly perpendicular to the surface the roller runs on. It cannot resist any force along that surface, so the beam can expand and contract freely.1 (size only)
Hinge (pin)A force in any direction. Find its horizontal and vertical components separately, then combine them.2 (size and direction)
Real bridges use a hinge at one end and a roller at the other. The hinge stops the bridge sliding off its supports; the roller lets the deck expand on a hot day and shrink on a cold one without pushing the abutments apart. The Queensferry Crossing has expansion joints and bearings that move by more than a metre between summer and winter.

Method

  1. Draw a free-body diagram. Resolve any angled loads into vertical and horizontal components.
  2. Take moments about the hinge (or about one support). This removes that support’s unknowns from the equation and gives the other reaction.
  3. Use ΣFV = 0 to find the vertical component at the hinge.
  4. Use ΣFH = 0 to find the horizontal component at the hinge.
  5. Combine the hinge components: magnitude by Pythagoras, direction by tan−1. State the direction on a sketch or in words.
Worked example 1 — simple supports with an overhang
1
Take moments about A. The 8.0 kN load is clockwise; RB and the overhanging 10 kN load are anticlockwise.
2
ΣCWM = ΣACWM:   8.0 × 0.8 = (RB × 1.6) + (10 × 0.5)  →  1.6RB = 6.4 − 5.0 = 1.4
3
RB = 1.4 / 1.6 = 0.88 kN (0.875 kN)
4
ΣFV = 0:   RA + RB = 10 + 8.0  →  RA = 18 − 0.875 = 17.125 kN = 17 kN
Worked example 2 — hinge and roller with an angled load
1
Resolve the 40 N load: vertical component 40 sin 30° = 20 N (down); horizontal component 40 cos 30° = 34.64 N (to the left).
2
Moments about the hinge A (the horizontal component passes through the beam’s line, so it has no moment):  RB × 8 = (20 × 4) + (20 × 6) = 200  →  RB = 25 N
3
ΣFV = 0:   RAV + 25 = 20 + 20  →  RAV = 15 N (up)
4
ΣFH = 0:   RAH = 34.64 N (to the right, balancing the load’s horizontal component). The roller cannot help with this.
5
RA = √(152 + 34.642) = 37.75… N = 38 N;   θ = tan−1(15 / 34.64) = 23° above the horizontal, acting up and to the right.

Section 7: Uniformly Distributed Loads

A uniformly distributed load (UDL) is spread evenly along a beam, rather than acting at one point. Examples are the weight of the beam itself, a floor slab, snow on a roof, water in a tank, or a crowd on a footbridge. A UDL is given as a load per metre, in N m−1 or kN m−1, and drawn as a row of humps.

For calculating reactions, a UDL can be replaced by a single equivalent point load:

Equivalent point load = w × length covered
It acts at the centre of the length the UDL covers
Partial UDLs. If the UDL covers only part of the beam, use only the length it covers, and place the point load at the centre of that part — not at the centre of the beam. Measure its distance from the point you take moments about carefully.
Worked example 1 — full-length UDL
1
Equivalent point load of the UDL: 0.75 × 5.7 = 4.275 kN, acting at the centre of the beam, 2.85 m from A.
2
Moments about A: RB × 5.7 = (6.3 × 1.5) + (4.275 × 2.85) = 9.45 + 12.18… = 21.63…
3
RB = 21.63… / 5.7 = 3.7954… kN = 3.8 kN
4
ΣFV = 0:   RA = 6.3 + 4.275 − 3.7954 = 6.7796… kN = 6.8 kN. There are no horizontal loads, so the hinge reaction is vertical.

Section 8: Frames: Reactions and Stability

A frame is made of straight members joined at nodes by pins. If loads are applied only at the nodes, every member is a strut or a tie: it carries force only along its length. Frames are built from triangles because a triangle cannot change shape unless a member changes length.

Is the frame stable?

For a pin-jointed plane frame with n nodes, m members and r reaction components (a hinge gives 2, a roller gives 1), compare 2n with m + r:

ResultMeaning
2n = m + rJust stable (statically determinate). Every force can be found by nodal analysis.
2n > m + rUnstable — a mechanism. Not enough members or supports: the frame will collapse or move.
2n < m + rRedundant (indeterminate). More members than needed. It is stiffer, but the forces cannot be found by nodal analysis alone.

NODEarama checks this for you when you press Solve: it reports Solved Stable, Solved (Indeterminate) or Unstable Mechanism.

Reactions on a frame

Treat the whole frame as a single rigid body and use the conditions of equilibrium, exactly as for a beam. Take moments about a hinge so that its two unknown components disappear. You may need trigonometry to find the perpendicular distance first.

Worked example — reaction at the roller
1
Find the distance X between the supports: tan 38° = X / 1.30, so X = 1.30 tan 38° = 1.0157… m.
2
The roller at B can only push perpendicular to the wall, so RB is horizontal. Take moments about the hinge A:
3
ΣCWM = ΣACWM:   2.25 × 1.30 = RB × 1.0157
4
RB = 2.925 / 1.0157 = 2.880… kN = 2.9 kN, acting horizontally away from the wall.
5
At the hinge: RAH = 2.880 kN (towards the wall) and RAV = 2.25 kN (up), so RA = 3.7 kN at 38.0° to the horizontal.

Section 9: Nodal Analysis

Every node in a frame in equilibrium is itself in equilibrium. So at each node, ΣFH = 0 and ΣFV = 0 — exactly as for the concurrent forces in Section 4. Working through the frame node by node, you can find the magnitude and the nature (strut or tie) of the force in every member. This is nodal analysis (also called the method of joints).

Method

  1. If the frame is supported like a beam, find the reactions first by taking moments for the whole frame. (For a frame fixed to a wall you can often start at the free end instead.)
  2. Choose a node with no more than two unknown member forces. Draw a free-body diagram of that node.
  3. Assume each unknown member is a tie (arrow pointing away from the node). Write ΣFV = 0 and ΣFH = 0 and solve.
  4. A positive answer means your assumption was right (tie). A negative answer means the member is a strut. Alternatively, decide the direction from the diagram, as in the worked examples below.
  5. Move to the next node, using the forces you have just found. Remember: at the other end of a member, the force has the same size and nature, so its arrow on the new node points the opposite way along the member.
  6. Record the results in a table: member, magnitude (with units), nature.
Worked example 1 — wall crane (start at the free end)
1
Node C has two unknowns, AC and BC. The only vertical forces are the 1000 N load and the vertical component of BC, so BC must push C upwards: BC is a strut.
ΣFV = 0:   BC sin 30° = 1000  →  BC = 1000 / sin 30° = 2000 N (strut)
2
BC pushes C up and to the right, so AC must pull C to the left, towards A: AC is a tie.
ΣFH = 0:   AC = BC cos 30° = 2000 × cos 30° = 1732 N = 1700 N (tie)
3
Node B: the strut BC now pushes B down and to the left (1000 N down, 1732 N left). The roller pushes B horizontally away from the wall, so RB = 1700 N. AB must pull B up with 1000 N: AB is a tie of 1000 N.
4
Node A (check and hinge reaction): the ties AC and AB pull A to the right (1732 N) and down (1000 N). The hinge must provide 1732 N towards the wall and 1000 N up: RA = √(17322 + 10002) = 2000 N at 30° to the horizontal.

Section 10: Two Unknowns at a Node: Simultaneous Equations

At some nodes, neither of the two unknown members is horizontal or vertical, so neither equation can be solved on its own. Both unknowns appear in ΣFH = 0 and in ΣFV = 0. Solve them as simultaneous equations by substitution:

  1. Write ΣFH = 0 and ΣFV = 0, with each force as force × cos or sin of its angle.
  2. Rearrange the simpler equation to make one unknown the subject (equation 1).
  3. Substitute equation 1 into the other equation, so it has only one unknown. Solve it.
  4. Put the answer back into equation 1 to find the second unknown.
  5. Check the signs: a negative answer means the force acts the other way to the one you assumed.
Worked example — two cables at different angles
1
ΣFH = 0:   M2 cos 17.5° = M1 cos 12.5°  →  M2 = M1 × cos 12.5° / cos 17.5° = 1.0237 M1  (equation 1)
2
ΣFV = 0:   M1 sin 12.5° + M2 sin 17.5° = 2.5  (equation 2)
3
Substitute: M1 sin 12.5° + 1.0237 M1 sin 17.5° = 2.5  →  (0.2164 + 0.3078) M1 = 2.5  →  0.5243 M1 = 2.5
4
M1 = 4.769… kN = 4.8 kN
5
From equation 1: M2 = 1.0237 × 4.769 = 4.881… kN = 4.9 kN
Notice how much larger the cable tensions (about 4.8 kN) are than the 2.5 kN load. Shallow cables need very large tensions to provide a small vertical force — the same reason a washing line can never be pulled perfectly straight.
