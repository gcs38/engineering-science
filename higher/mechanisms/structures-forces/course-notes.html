Section 1: Structures and Loads

A structure is anything designed to carry a load and transfer it safely to the ground (or to another structure). Engineers must be able to calculate the forces inside a structure before it is built, so that every part can be made strong enough — and no stronger than it needs to be, because extra material costs money and adds weight.

Frames and beams

At Higher you analyse two kinds of structure. A frame is made of straight members joined at their ends at nodes (pin joints). Frames are built from triangles, because a triangle cannot change shape unless one of its members changes length. A beam is a member that carries loads across a gap, such as a walkway or a bridge deck.

Loads

Loads are forces, measured in newtons (N), kilonewtons (kN, × 103) or meganewtons (MN, × 106). Weight is a force. If you are given a mass in kg, find its weight with W = mg. The data booklet gives the gravitational acceleration as g = 9.8 ms−2, so a mass of 1.0 kg has a weight of 9.8 N. Never write a mass on a force diagram: change it to a weight first.

Loads can be static (they do not change, such as the weight of a bridge deck) or dynamic (they change or move, such as wind or traffic). You will use these again with factor of safety in the Materials notes.

Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram shows one structure, or one part of it such as a single node, on its own, with every force acting on it — the loads and the reactions from the supports — drawn as arrows, with the angles and distances you need. Drawing a clear free-body diagram is always the first step, and you must be able to draw them for whole structures (beams and frames) and for single nodes.

Reactions

A reaction is the force a support exerts on a structure to hold it up and keep it still. When a load presses a beam down onto its supports, the supports push back up on the beam; when a frame is fixed to a wall, the wall pushes or pulls on the frame where it is fixed. Every load has to be carried to the ground through the reactions. In equilibrium, the reactions exactly balance the loads, so the structure does not move. Reactions are usually labelled R with the name of the support, such as RA and RB.

Tension first. Sections 2 to 4 deal with forces at a point from cables, chains, ropes and slings. These can only be in tension: a cable always pulls away from the point it is attached to, along the line of the cable — it can never push. Members that can also push (struts, in compression) come later, in Section 8.

Section 2: Resolving Forces

A force is a vector: it has a size (its magnitude) and a direction. It is drawn as an arrow: the length of the arrow shows the size, and the angle shows the direction.

Two forces at right angles

Start with a horizontal force of 80 N and a vertical force of 60 N pulling on the same point. Together they have exactly the same effect as one single force, called the resultant. To find it, draw the two arrows tip to tail: they make a right-angled triangle, and the resultant is the hypotenuse.

R = √802 + 602 = 100 N      tan θ = 6080 → θ = 37°

So the two forces can be replaced by one 100 N force at 37° above the horizontal. Resolving a force is the same idea worked backwards.

Resolving a force into components

Resolving a force means replacing it with two forces at right angles — a horizontal component FH and a vertical component FV — that together have exactly the same effect as the original force.

FH = 90 cos 30°      FV = 90 sin 30°

These come straight from trigonometry (SOH CAH TOA). The force is the hypotenuse, FH is adjacent to the 30° angle and FV is opposite it. In general, for a force F at an angle θ to the horizontal: FH = F cos θ and FV = F sin θ.

Worked example — resolving a 90 N force

Resolve this 90 N force, which acts down and to the left at 30° below the horizontal.

Horizontal component
FH = 90 cos 30°
FH = 77.94…
FH = 78 N to the left
Vertical component
FV = 90 sin 30°
FV = 45 N downwards
Check
F = √77.942 + 452 = 90 N ✓
Check which angle you are given. If the angle is measured from the vertical, the components swap: FH = F sin θ and FV = F cos θ. Always ask yourself: is the component adjacent to the angle, or opposite it? Exam diagrams use both.

From components back to a single force

If you know both components, Pythagoras gives the size of the force and tan gives its angle to the horizontal:

F = √FH2 + FV2      tan θ = FVFH

Always state the direction in words as well as the angle, for example “38° above the horizontal, to the left”, or mark it on a sketch.

Section 3: Equilibrium of Concurrent Forces

Conditions for equilibrium

A structure, or any part of it, is in static equilibrium when it is at rest and stays at rest. Three conditions must all be met. They are given in the data booklet:

ΣFH = 0      ΣFV = 0      ΣM = 0
Condition (data booklet)What it meansHow to write it in your working
ΣFH = 0The horizontal forces balance: no sideways movementΣF← = ΣF→
ΣFV = 0The vertical forces balance: no upward or downward movementΣF↑ = ΣF↓
ΣM = 0The moments balance: no turningΣCWM = ΣACWM

Concurrent forces all act through one point — for example the forces on a node, or on a lifting ring. Concurrent forces cannot turn the point, because every force passes through it and so has no moment about it. ΣM = 0 is therefore always true for concurrent forces and is not needed: you use only ΣFH = 0 and ΣFV = 0. Two equations means you can find at most two unknowns at a point.

Writing the equations. In your working, write ΣFV = 0 as ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ and ΣFH = 0 as ΣF← = ΣF→: the forces acting one way on one side, the forces acting the other way on the other side, each as force × sin or cos of its angle. It means exactly the same as the data booklet form, and there are no minus signs to lose.

Resultant and equilibrant

When several forces act on a point, they combine into a single resultant force. We usually balance this resultant with an equal and opposite force called the equilibrant. Exam questions ask for it as “the force required to maintain equilibrium”: treat it as an unknown force R at an unknown angle θ, and find it with the equilibrium conditions.

Method

  1. Draw a free-body diagram of the point, including the unknown force R at an angle θ.
  2. Resolve every known force into horizontal and vertical components.
  3. Use ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ to find RV, the vertical component of R.
  4. Use ΣF← = ΣF→ to find RH, the horizontal component of R.
  5. Combine them: R = √RH2 + RV2 and tan θ = RV / RH. State the direction.
Worked example — the force needed for equilibrium

The point is in equilibrium. Find the magnitude and direction of the unknown force R.

ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
(600 sin 50°) = (250 sin 35°) + RV
RV = 316.23… N (downwards)
ΣF← = ΣF→
RH = (600 cos 50°) + (250 cos 35°)
RH = 590.46… N (to the left)
Combine the components
R = √316.232 + 590.462
R = 670 N
tan θ = 316.23590.46
θ = 28° below the horizontal, to the left
Force triangle. To picture a resultant, draw its horizontal and vertical components tip to tail, as in Section 2 and the last step above: they make a right-angled triangle with the resultant as the hypotenuse. You only ever need right-angled triangles (Pythagoras and tan).

Section 4: Finding Unknown Forces One at a Time

Often the directions of the unknown forces are known — they act along cables, chains or slings — but not their sizes. If one of the two unknowns is horizontal (or vertical), one of the equilibrium equations contains only the other unknown. Solve that equation first, then use the answer in the second equation.

Worked example — a lamp held by two cables

A 200 N lamp hangs from a ring held by cable A, at 50° to the horizontal, and the horizontal cable B. Find the tension in each cable.

ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ (only TA has a vertical component)
TA sin 50° = 200
TA = 200sin 50°
TA = 261.08… N
TA = 260 N
ΣF← = ΣF→
TA cos 50° = TB
TB = 261.08… × cos 50°
TB = 167.82… N
TB = 170 N
Symmetry. When a load hangs from two equal slings at the same angle, the two tensions are equal, so ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ alone gives the answer: 2T cos θ = W, where θ is the angle of each sling from the vertical.

Section 5: Moments and Beam Reactions

Moments: a reminder

A force that does not pass through a point has a turning effect, or moment, about that point. Moments are measured in Nm or kNm:

Moment = force × perpendicular distance

The forces on a beam do not all act through one point, so all three equilibrium conditions are needed. The principle of moments, ΣM = 0, is written ΣCWM = ΣACWM: the sum of the clockwise moments equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments, about any point. Take moments about a support, so that its unknown reaction has no moment and drops out of the equation.

Worked example 1 — vertical loads only
Moments about A
ΣCWM = ΣACWM
(3.0 × 1.0) + (5.0 × 3.0) = RB × 4.0
18 = 4.0RB
RB = 4.5 kN
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
RA + RB = 3.0 + 5.0
RA = 8.0 − 4.5
RA = 3.5 kN
Worked example 2 — an overhanging load
Moments about A
The 8.0 kN load is clockwise about A; RB and the overhanging 10 kN load are anticlockwise.
ΣCWM = ΣACWM
8.0 × 0.80 = (RB × 1.6) + (10 × 0.50)
1.6RB = 6.4 − 5.0 = 1.4
RB = 0.875 kN → RB = 0.88 kN
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
RA + RB = 10 + 8.0
RA = 18 − 0.875 = 17.125 kN → RA = 17 kN

Types of support

SupportReaction it can provideUnknowns
Simple supportActs only vertically upwards.1
RollerActs only at right angles to the surface it rolls on. It cannot resist any force along that surface, so the beam can expand and contract freely.1 (size only)
Hinge (pin)Can act in any direction. Find its horizontal component RH and vertical component RV separately, then combine them.2 (size and direction)

Exam questions usually use a hinge at one end and a roller at the other, as real bridges do. The hinge stops the beam sliding off its supports; the roller lets it expand on a hot day and shrink on a cold one without pushing the supports apart.

Forces at an angle

When a force acts at an angle to a beam, resolve it into a component at right angles to the beam and a component along the beam. Only the component at right angles to the beam has a moment about a point on the beam: the component along the beam passes through the point. But the component along the beam still has to be balanced — by the horizontal reaction at the hinge, because a roller cannot provide one.

The two worked examples below use the same bar, hinged at O, with a 150 N load. First the force F at the end is vertical; then it acts at 60° to the bar.

Worked example 3 — a vertical force at the end
Moments about O
ΣCWM = ΣACWM
150 × 1.0 = F × 2.4
F = 62.5 N → F = 63 N
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
RV + F = 150
RV = 150 − 62.5 = 87.5 N → RV = 88 N upwards
There are no horizontal forces, so the hinge reaction is vertical.
Worked example 4 — the same force at an angle
Moments about O
Only F sin 60° has a moment about O; F cos 60° acts along the bar, through O.
150 × 1.0 = F sin 60° × 2.4
F = 150sin 60° × 2.4 = 72.17… N → F = 72 N
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
RV + F sin 60° = 150
RV = 150 − 62.5 = 87.5 N (upwards)
ΣF← = ΣF→
RH = F cos 60°
RH = 72.17… × cos 60° = 36.08… N (to the right)
Combine the hinge components
R = √87.52 + 36.082
R = 95 N
tan θ = 87.536.08
θ = 68° above the horizontal

Angling the force makes F bigger (72 N instead of 63 N) for the same moment, and the hinge now has to supply a horizontal component as well.

Section 6: Uniformly Distributed Loads

A uniformly distributed load (UDL) is spread evenly along a beam, rather than acting at one point. Examples are the weight of the beam itself, a floor slab, snow on a roof, water in a tank, or a crowd on a footbridge. A UDL is given as a load per metre, in Nm−1 or kNm−1, and drawn as a row of humps.

For calculating reactions, a UDL can be replaced by a single equivalent point load:

Equivalent point load = w × length covered
It acts at the centre of the length the UDL covers
Partial UDLs. If the UDL covers only part of the beam, use only the length it covers, and place the point load at the centre of that part — not at the centre of the beam. Measure its distance from the point you take moments about carefully.
Worked example 1 — full-length UDL
Equivalent point load
0.75 × 5.7 = 4.275 kN
It acts at the centre of the beam, 2.85 m from A.
Moments about A
ΣCWM = ΣACWM
(6.3 × 1.5) + (4.275 × 2.85) = RB × 5.7
21.63… = 5.7RB
RB = 3.7954… kN → RB = 3.8 kN
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
RA + RB = 6.3 + 4.275
RA = 6.7796… kN → RA = 6.8 kN
There are no horizontal loads, so the hinge reaction is vertical.

Section 7: Reactions on Frames

A whole frame in equilibrium can be treated as one rigid body, exactly like a beam. You can take moments about any point, but it is best to take moments about a hinge. Moment = force × perpendicular distance from the chosen point, and the hinge’s two unknown reaction components act through the hinge, so their distance from it is zero and their moments are zero. They do not appear in the moment equation at all. That leaves only one unknown — the reaction at the other support — which can then be calculated easily. You may need trigonometry to find the perpendicular distance first. Then use ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ and ΣF← = ΣF→ to find the hinge reaction.

Worked example — reactions on a wall-mounted frame
Directions of the reactions
Start by replacing the supports with their reactions, as in the free-body diagram of the whole frame above.
RB (roller): a roller can only push at right angles to the surface it rests on. The wall is vertical, so RB is horizontal. The load at C tries to turn the frame clockwise about A, which presses B into the wall, so the wall pushes B away from the wall.
RAH (hinge, horizontal): RB pushes the frame away from the wall, so for ΣF← = ΣF→ the hinge must pull A towards the wall. The top of the frame is trying to pull away from the wall.
RAV (hinge, vertical): the roller cannot provide any vertical force, so the hinge carries the whole 2.25 kN load: RAV acts upwards.
RA (dashed) is the single force made by combining RAH and RAV: it acts up and towards the wall at an angle θ.
Distance between the supports
tan 38° = X1.30
X = 1.30 tan 38° = 1.0157… m
Moments about the hinge A
The roller at B can only push at right angles to the wall, so RB is horizontal.
ΣCWM = ΣACWM
2.25 × 1.30 = RB × 1.0157…
RB = 2.880… kN → RB = 2.9 kN, acting horizontally away from the wall
Hinge reaction
ΣF← = ΣF→: RAH = 2.880 kN (towards the wall)
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓: RAV = 2.25 kN (upwards)

Section 8: Struts, Ties and Nodal Analysis

Struts and ties

If the loads on a frame act only at its nodes, every member is either being stretched or squashed:

  • A tie is a member in tension. The loads try to stretch it, so it pulls inwards on the nodes at each end.
  • A strut is a member in compression. The loads try to shorten it, so it pushes outwards on the nodes at each end.

The force a member exerts on the node at each end is an internal reaction force: the member reacts to being stretched or squashed. That is the force you draw on a node’s free-body diagram.

At a node: draw a tie as an arrow pointing away from the node, into the member (it pulls the node along the member), and a strut as an arrow pointing towards the node (it pushes the node). You will use this in every nodal analysis question.

Starting with a single node

A node in a frame is just a point with concurrent forces acting on it — exactly like the points in Sections 3 and 4. Draw its free-body diagram, then use ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ and ΣF← = ΣF→.

Worked example — one node

A wall bracket carries a 600 N load at C. Find the magnitude and nature of the forces in AC and BC.

Free-body diagram of node C
The 600 N load acts down. AC is horizontal; BC is at 40° to the horizontal.
Only BC can balance the load vertically, so BC must push C upwards: BC is a strut.
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
BC sin 40° = 600
BC = 600sin 40° = 933.43… N
BC = 930 N (strut)
ΣF← = ΣF→
BC pushes C to the right, so AC must pull C to the left, towards the wall: AC is a tie.
AC = BC cos 40° = 933.43… × cos 40°
AC = 720 N (tie)

Nodal analysis of a whole frame

Every node in a frame in equilibrium is itself in equilibrium. Working through the frame node by node, you can find the magnitude and the nature (strut or tie) of the force in every member. This is nodal analysis.

  1. If the frame is supported like a beam, find the reactions first by taking moments for the whole frame. (For a frame fixed to a wall you can often start at the free end instead.)
  2. Choose a node with no more than two unknown member forces, and preferably one where an unknown is horizontal or vertical. Draw its free-body diagram.
  3. Use ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ and ΣF← = ΣF→ to find the unknowns, and decide from the directions whether each member is a strut or a tie.
  4. Move to the next node, using the forces you have just found. At the other end of a member the force has the same size and nature, so its arrow on the new node points the opposite way along the member.
  5. Record the results in a table: member, magnitude (with units), nature.

Section 9: Two Unknowns at a Node: Simultaneous Equations

At some nodes neither of the two unknown forces is horizontal or vertical, so both unknowns appear in ΣF↑ = ΣF↓ and in ΣF← = ΣF→. Neither equation can be solved on its own: they are simultaneous equations. Leave these until you are confident with the earlier sections.

Any method from Maths is fine. Whatever method you use for simultaneous equations in Maths will earn the marks. These notes show substitution, which is the method named in the course specification, and then an alternative, the revolve method, which avoids simultaneous equations altogether.

Substitution

  1. Write ΣF← = ΣF→ and ΣF↑ = ΣF↓, with each force as force × cos or sin of its angle.
  2. Rearrange the simpler equation to make one unknown the subject (equation 1).
  3. Substitute equation 1 into the other equation, so that it has only one unknown. Solve it.
  4. Put the answer back into equation 1 to find the second unknown.
Worked example 1 — tension in two cables (substitution)
ΣF← = ΣF→
T1 cos 30° = T2 cos 45°
T2 = T1 cos 30°cos 45° = 1.2247 T1   (equation 1)
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
T1 sin 30° + T2 sin 45° = 25   (equation 2)
Substitute equation 1 into equation 2
T1 sin 30° + (1.2247 T1) sin 45° = 25
1.3660 T1 = 25
T1 = 18.30… kN → T1 = 18 kN
T2 = 1.2247 × 18.30… = 22.41… kN → T2 = 22 kN
Keep the unrounded multiplier. Rounding T2 = 1.2247T1 to 1.2T1 part-way through can change your final answer. Store values in your calculator’s memory.

The revolve method

Horizontal and vertical are not the only directions you can resolve in. If you resolve at right angles to one of the unknown forces, that force has no component in that direction, so it drops out. You are left with one equation and one unknown. In effect you “revolve” the diagram until one unknown lies along an axis.

Worked example 2 — the same cables by the revolve method
Resolve at right angles to T2 (along the purple line)
T2 has no component along this line.
T1 is 15° from the line (45° + 90° = 135°, and 150° − 135° = 15°); the 25 kN load is 45° from it.
T1 cos 15° = 25 cos 45°
T1 = 25 cos 45°cos 15° = 18.30… kN → T1 = 18 kN
Now T1 is known: ΣF← = ΣF→
T2 cos 45° = T1 cos 30°
T2 = 18.30… × cos 30°cos 45° = 22.41… kN → T2 = 22 kN

The answers are the same as by substitution. The tricky part of the revolve method is finding the angles to the new direction, so always sketch it.

Worked example 3 — two cables at shallow angles
ΣF← = ΣF→
M2 cos 17.5° = M1 cos 12.5°
M2 = 1.0237 M1   (equation 1)
ΣF↑ = ΣF↓
M1 sin 12.5° + M2 sin 17.5° = 2.5   (equation 2)
Substitute
M1 sin 12.5° + 1.0237 M1 sin 17.5° = 2.5
0.5243 M1 = 2.5
M1 = 4.769… kN → M1 = 4.8 kN
M2 = 1.0237 × 4.769… = 4.881… kN → M2 = 4.9 kN

Notice how much larger the cable tensions are than the 2.5 kN load. Shallow cables need very large tensions to give a small vertical force — the same reason a washing line can never be pulled perfectly straight.
