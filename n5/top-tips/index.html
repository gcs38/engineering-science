🔢 Significant figures

Rounding your final answer is expected — but there is a generous tolerance.

Round your final answer to a sensible number of significant figures. In recent National 5 exams the marking schemes almost always give the expected answer to 2 significant figures, and accept anything from one figure fewer to two figures more.

So if the expected answer is given to 2 significant figures, answers with 1, 2, 3 or 4 significant figures get the mark (if the value is right). Copying down every digit on your calculator can lose the mark, because it has far more than two extra figures.

Counting significant figures

  • Start counting at the first non-zero digit. Zeros in front don’t count: 0.018 A has 2 s.f.
  • Zeros between other digits count: 4.07 V has 3 s.f.
  • Zeros at the end of a whole number are unclear: is 210 000 J two figures or six? Writing 210 kJ or 2.1 × 105 J makes it clear — another good reason to use prefixes.
  • To round, look at the next digit: 5 or more, round up; 4 or less, leave it.

How many should I use?

Give your final answer to 2 significant figures. Almost all exam questions give their data to 2 significant figures, and good practice is to give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data. It also matches the recent marking schemes and sits in the middle of the accepted window. If a question asks for a specific number of figures or decimal places, do exactly what it asks.

Watch out for whole numbers: 3 m to 2 significant figures is 3.0 m, and 0.5 A is 0.50 A.

⏱️ When to round

Round the final answer only. Keep the full calculator value for every step in between.

The marking schemes carry the full calculator value through each stage, and only round at the end. If you round too early, your final answer can drift outside the accepted range.

  • Use your calculator’s Ans key or memory to carry values forward rather than retyping a rounded number.
  • If you do write an intermediate value down, write it to at least 4 significant figures.
  • Use the π button on your calculator, not 3.14. The course reports say this gives more accurate answers, and 3.14 can push an answer outside the accepted range.
  • If you make an error (or round) early, you can still earn marks for later steps that are done correctly using your value — markers apply “follow-through”. But it is safer not to rely on it.

📏 Units

A final answer needs its unit. Marks are usually given for “final answer with unit” — a correct number with no unit, or the wrong unit, may not get the mark.
  • Any clear format is accepted: m s−1, m/s or metres per second.
  • Some quantities are ratios and have no unit: efficiency, velocity ratio, gear ratio and strain.
  • Rotational speed: write rev min−1 or rev s−1. Marking schemes have specifically said do not accept RPM.
  • Pressure and stress are written Nmm−2, which is how the data booklet writes them. It means exactly the same as N/mm2, newtons per square millimetre: the power −2 means “per mm2”, just as s−1 means “per second”. So a cylinder supplied at 0.50 Nmm−2 has 0.50 N pushing on every square millimetre of the piston.
  • For pressure and stress, Nmm−2 is the expected unit. You don’t need to convert areas to m2 unless the question asks for N m−2 or Pa. If you do use N m−2 or Pa, you must convert the area to m2 first. The course reports mention this mistake every year.
  • Use the method in the data booklet for gear trains and speeds. The 2024 course report picked this out.
  • Remember that an idler gear changes the direction of rotation but not the output speed.

Capital or small letter matters. M (mega, a million) and m (milli, a thousandth) differ by a factor of a billion. A 2.2 MΩ resistor is not a 2.2 mΩ resistor!

Converting

  • Big unit → small unit: multiply. 4.7 kΩ = 4.7 × 1000 = 4700 Ω.
  • Small unit → big unit: divide. 18 mA = 181000 = 0.018 A.
  • On a calculator, type 4.7 kΩ as 4.7×103 (the ×10x or EXP key). The ENG key shows answers in steps of 103, which match the prefixes.
  • Squared units square the prefix: 1 m = 1000 mm, but 1 m2 = 1 000 000 mm2. 1 Nmm−2 = 1 MPa.

Shortcuts that keep the prefixes

Some prefix pairs work together, so you can skip the conversion: volts divided by kΩ gives mA, and mA × kΩ = V. For example, 12 V4.7 kΩ = 2.55 mA. If in doubt, convert everything to base units first.

✍️ Showing your working

Unless a question asks you to show working, a correct final answer with its unit gets full marks on its own. But if the final answer is wrong, working is the only way to pick up marks — marking schemes give marks for the steps along the way.

  1. Write the formula (from the data booklet, if it is there).
  2. Substitute the numbers (in consistent units).
  3. Rearrange (transpose) if needed.
  4. Give the final answer, rounded, with its unit.

If a separate box is given for the final answer, only that box is marked — make sure your answer is in it.

💬 Describe and explain answers

Written answers lose more marks than calculations. Every recent National 5 course report says that answers were not detailed enough.

Each mark needs its own clear point. Use the labels on the diagram, use the correct engineering words, and link everything to the context in the question.

“Explain”: give a cause and its effect

Give one cause that fits the context, and the effect it leads to. Giving two causes, or a cause with an effect that doesn’t follow from it, does not get full marks. For example, an advantage of simulation: “the circuit can be tested before it is built (cause), so no components are wasted if it doesn’t work (effect)”.

“Describe”: take the reader through it step by step

  • Name each component by its label (R1, V3, CA, the LDR) and say what it does at each step.
  • Follow the chain from input to output. For example: the light level falls, so the LDR’s resistance rises, so Vin rises, so when Vin reaches 0.7 V the transistor switches on and the lamp lights. The 2026 course report picked out the LDR’s resistance and Vin as the steps that were often missed.
  • Say what changes and which way. “The resistance increases” or “Vout decreases”, not just “it changes”.
  • Pneumatics: learn the names of the symbols (main air, exhaust, pilot air, diaphragm and other actuators). 3/2 valves actuate and unactuate, 5/2 valves change state, pilot air is sent or cut off, and cylinders outstroke or instroke. “A goes out and hits a valve” gets no marks.
  • Systems: say whether the system is open or closed loop. A motor or other part inside the system is not an input or an output of the whole system. For closed loop, say that the sensor measures the actual output and the control sub-system compares it with the set value.

Roles of engineers

  • Give two different tasks, each one specific to the context. “Design the structure” or “develop the circuit” are too general to get the mark.
  • Know the difference between an electrical engineer (power supply, cabling, distribution) and an electronic engineer (control circuits, sensors, microcontrollers).

Emerging technologies

An emerging technology must be new and not yet in commercial use. Technologies that are already widely sold, such as electric vehicles, have not been accepted.

Impacts

Link your answer to the cause. For example, climate change is linked to greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide released when fossil fuels are burned; just saying “pollution” is not enough.

✅ Exam checklist

  • Kept the full calculator value until the end?
  • Rounded the final answer — 2 significant figures is a safe default?
  • Unit included, with the right prefix (and capital/small letter)?
  • Ratio answers (efficiency, velocity ratio) left without a unit?
  • Used the π button rather than 3.14?
  • Written answers: labels used, correct terms, one clear point for each mark, values quoted, linked to the context?
  • Does the answer make sense? (An efficiency of 140%, or a gear that turns faster than the motor when it should be slower, means something has gone wrong.)
