Section 1: What is Pneumatics?

Pneumatics is the use of pressurised air to do mechanical work. A pneumatic system takes air from the atmosphere, compresses it, stores it, and then controls its flow to produce linear or rotary movement.

Key point: Pneumatic systems are widely used in industry because compressed air is safe, clean, and readily available. Common applications include automated assembly lines, door openers, dental drills, and bus door mechanisms.

Advantages of pneumatic systems

Pneumatic systems offer several advantages over hydraulic or electrical systems: compressed air is non-flammable and safe to use in hazardous environments; the components are relatively low-cost; speed is easy to adjust using flow control valves; and any leaks result only in air escaping rather than fluid spillage.

Disadvantages

Air is compressible, so precise positioning can be difficult. Pneumatic systems can be noisy, and energy is lost as heat when air is compressed. A compressor and air supply are always required.

Section 2: Pneumatic Components and Symbols

All pneumatic circuits are drawn using standard BS/ISO symbols. You need to be able to recognise each component symbol, know its function, and identify how it is connected in a circuit.

Mains air supply and exhaust

Every pneumatic circuit needs a mains air supply — the pressurised air from the compressor. In circuit diagrams this is shown as a filled triangle pointing into the circuit. Exhaust is where used air vents to atmosphere after passing through the circuit; it is shown as an open triangle pointing away from the circuit.

Mains air supply
Pressurised air entering the circuit. Always connects to port 1 of a valve.
Exhaust
Used air venting to atmosphere. Always on ports 3 and 5 of a valve.
Reading a circuit diagram: Mains air always enters at port 1 (the bottom of a valve symbol). Exhaust ports vent used air to atmosphere — look for the open triangle. When a valve switches, the mains air path changes and the opposite side of the cylinder exhausts.

Reservoir (capacitance vessel)

A reservoir stores a volume of compressed air. This smooths out pressure fluctuations and ensures a steady supply is always available. Reservoirs are also a key component in time delay circuits, where they fill slowly through a restrictor to create a timed delay before a valve switches.

Directional control valves (DCVs)

Directional control valves direct mains air to different parts of the circuit. They are described by the number of ports (connections) and the number of positions (states the valve can be in).

How to read a valve symbol

A DCV symbol is drawn as a row of boxes — one box for each position. Each box shows the internal flow paths when the valve is in that position: arrows show where air can flow, and blocked T-shapes show where it cannot. The box that is currently active (the valve's present state) is the one aligned with the port lines on the outside of the symbol.

At rest (un-actuated), the right-hand box is active — this is the spring-return (normal) position. When the valve is actuated, the left-hand box shifts into the active position, connecting mains air to the output.

3/2 valve — used with single acting cylinders

A 3/2 valve has 3 ports and 2 positions. Port 1 = mains air in; port 2 = air to/from cylinder; port 3 = exhaust. In the normal position, the cylinder is connected to exhaust. When actuated, mains air flows through to the cylinder.

Normal position — at rest
Port 2 (cylinder) connects to port 3 (exhaust). Mains air at port 1 is blocked. The cylinder spring holds the rod retracted.
Actuated — button pressed
Mains air at port 1 connects to port 2 (cylinder). Port 3 (exhaust) is blocked. Pressurised air extends the cylinder piston.

5/2 valve — used with double acting cylinders

A 5/2 valve has 5 ports and 2 positions. Port 1 = mains air; port 2 = extend side of cylinder; port 3 = exhaust (extend side); port 4 = retract side of cylinder; port 5 = exhaust (retract side). Switching the valve reverses which side of the cylinder receives mains air.

Normal position — at rest
Mains air (port 1) flows to port 4 (retract side). Port 2 (extend side) exhausts through port 3. Cylinder rod is held retracted.
Actuated position
Mains air (port 1) flows to port 2 (extend side). Port 4 (retract side) exhausts through port 5. Cylinder rod extends.

Actuators — cylinders

Single acting cylinder (SAC): Mains air extends the piston rod. A spring returns it when pressure is released. SACs are used where force is only needed in one direction.

Double acting cylinder (DAC): Mains air drives both extension and retraction via two ports. DACs provide force in both directions.

Valve actuators — diaphragm and solenoid

A valve can be switched in different ways. The two types you need to know for N5 are:

Diaphragm (pilot) actuator: A pneumatic signal (mains air from another part of the circuit) pushes against a flexible diaphragm to move the valve spool. Used in semi-automatic and automatic circuits where one valve triggers another.

Solenoid actuator: An electrical coil creates a magnetic force that moves the valve spool when energised. This allows electronic or microcontroller-based systems to control pneumatic circuits.

Diaphragm (pilot) actuator
Pneumatic signal switches the valve — used in automatic circuits
Solenoid actuator
Electrical signal switches the valve — used in electronically controlled systems

Flow control valves

Flow control valves limit the flow rate of air, controlling the speed of a cylinder. There are two types:

Restrictor: Reduces flow equally in both directions — slows the cylinder in both extension and retraction.

Uni-directional restrictor: Contains a needle valve in parallel with a check valve. It allows free flow in one direction and restricted flow in the other — so extension and retraction speeds can be controlled independently.

Restrictor
Reduces flow in both directions equally
Uni-directional restrictor
Free flow one way, restricted flow the other

Shuttle valve (OR valve)

A shuttle valve has two inlets and one outlet. Mains air entering either inlet pushes the ball across to seal the other inlet and passes through to the outlet. This is a logical OR function — mains air passes through if input A or input B is pressurised.

T-piece (air supply splitter)

A T-piece splits one mains air supply into two branches, allowing the same supply to feed two different parts of a circuit simultaneously.

Section 3: Single Acting Cylinder Circuit

The simplest pneumatic circuit uses a single acting cylinder controlled by a 3/2 push-button valve. When the button is pressed, mains air flows to extend the cylinder. When released, the spring in the valve returns it to normal, exhausting the cylinder so the cylinder spring can retract the rod.

Worked Example — How the SAC circuit operates
1
At rest: The 3/2 valve is in its normal (spring-return) position. Port 1 (mains air) is blocked; port 2 (cylinder) is connected to port 3 (exhaust). The cylinder spring holds the rod retracted.
2
Button pressed: The valve shifts to its actuated position. Mains air at port 1 now connects to port 2 (cylinder). Port 3 (exhaust) is blocked. Pressurised air extends the piston rod against the spring.
3
Button released: The valve spring returns it to normal. Port 2 connects back to port 3 (exhaust). Air vents to atmosphere and the cylinder spring retracts the rod.

Section 4: Double Acting Cylinder Circuit

A double acting cylinder (DAC) requires mains air to be directed to either side of the piston, while the opposite side exhausts. This is controlled by a 5/2 directional control valve. Switching the valve reverses the direction of the cylinder.

DAC advantage over SAC: Because mains air actively drives retraction as well as extension, a DAC can produce a pushing force in both directions. The retraction force is slightly less than the extension force because the piston rod reduces the effective area on the retract side.

Section 5: T-Piece — Splitting the Air Supply

A T-piece connector allows one mains air supply to feed two or more separate branches of a circuit simultaneously. Both branches receive the same supply pressure. T-pieces are used when a single air source needs to power multiple actuators or valves at the same time.

Section 6: Shuttle Valve (OR Valve)

A shuttle valve (also called an OR valve) allows a cylinder to be operated from two separate control valves — mains air from either valve can extend the cylinder. This is useful when a machine needs to be operated from two different locations.

Inside the shuttle valve is a small ball. When mains air enters from one inlet, it pushes the ball to seal the other inlet and passes through to the outlet. If mains air enters the other inlet instead, the ball moves across and the same thing happens.

Logic function: A shuttle valve performs a logical OR operation. The cylinder extends if valve A or valve B supplies mains air. This is useful for two-handed operation or remote control of a machine.

Section 7: Speed Control — Flow Control Valves

The speed of a pneumatic cylinder can be controlled by limiting the flow rate of air. A uni-directional restrictor contains a variable needle valve in parallel with a check (non-return) valve.

Meter-in: The restrictor is placed on the mains air supply side. It limits the mains air flowing into the cylinder, slowing the stroke.

Meter-out: The restrictor is placed on the exhaust side. It limits the air flowing out to exhaust. This gives smoother speed control and is preferred in most applications because the back-pressure helps support the load.

Section 8: Semi-Automatic Circuit

A semi-automatic circuit requires a manual input to start each cycle, but part of the operation happens automatically. A typical arrangement uses a DAC, a 5/2 valve with diaphragm (pilot) actuators, and a roller trip valve triggered by the cylinder at the end of its stroke.

The operator presses a start button which sends a pilot signal (a small mains air pressure) to switch the 5/2 valve. Mains air extends the DAC. At full extension the rod trips the limit valve, which sends a pilot signal to switch the 5/2 valve back. Mains air now drives retraction while the extend side exhausts. The circuit then waits for the next button press.

Worked Example — Semi-automatic cycle
1
Start: Operator presses push-button → mains air pilots the 5/2 valve into its actuated position → mains air flows to the extend side (port 2) of the DAC → cylinder outstroke begins.
2
End of outstroke: Rod trips the roller limit valve → mains air pilots the 5/2 valve back to normal → mains air flows to retract side (port 4) → extend side exhausts through port 3 → cylinder instroke begins.
3
Wait: Cylinder fully retracted. Circuit waits for operator to press button again to start the next cycle.

Section 9: Fully Automatic Circuit

A fully automatic circuit repeats its cycle continuously without any manual input after the initial start signal. A second limit valve at the retracted position sends a pilot signal to trigger the next outstroke automatically, so the cylinder cycles back and forth until a stop signal is given.

Fully automatic vs semi-automatic: In a semi-automatic circuit, only one limit valve is used so the cylinder stops and waits at the retracted position. In a fully automatic circuit, a second limit valve at the retracted end immediately sends a pilot signal to trigger the next outstroke.

Section 10: Time Delay Circuit

A time delay valve introduces a controllable delay between an input signal and an output action. It consists of a uni-directional restrictor feeding a reservoir, which builds up mains air pressure slowly until it is high enough to pilot (switch) a 3/2 output valve.

By adjusting the restrictor, the fill time of the reservoir — and therefore the length of the delay — can be changed. When the input signal is removed, the reservoir vents quickly back through the check valve in the restrictor and the timer resets immediately.

Worked Example — Time delay operation
1
Input signal received: Mains air flows slowly through the restrictor, gradually filling the reservoir.
2
During delay: Reservoir pressure is below the pilot threshold — the 3/2 output valve stays in its normal position. No mains air reaches the actuator yet.
3
Delay complete: Reservoir pressure reaches the pilot threshold — the 3/2 valve switches and mains air flows through to the actuator.
4
Reset: When the input signal is removed, the reservoir vents quickly through the check valve and the timer resets ready for the next cycle.
