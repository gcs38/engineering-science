Section 1: What is Pneumatics?

Pneumatics is the use of pressurised air to do mechanical work. A pneumatic system takes air from the atmosphere, compresses it, stores it, and then controls its flow to produce linear or rotary movement.

Key point: Pneumatic systems are widely used in industry because compressed air is safe, clean, and readily available. Common applications include automated assembly lines, door openers, dental drills, and bus door mechanisms.

Advantages of pneumatic systems

Pneumatic systems offer several advantages over hydraulic or electrical systems: compressed air is non-flammable and safe to use in hazardous environments; the components are relatively low-cost; speed is easy to adjust using flow control valves; and any leaks result only in air escaping rather than fluid spillage.

Disadvantages

Air is compressible, so precise positioning can be difficult. Pneumatic systems can be noisy, and energy is lost as heat when air is compressed. A compressor and air supply are always required.

Section 2: Pneumatic Components

A pneumatic circuit is made up of several standard components. You need to be able to recognise each component, know its function, and draw its correct BS/ISO symbol.

The compressor

The compressor draws in atmospheric air and increases its pressure. Most workshop compressors compress air to around 6–8 bar (six to eight times atmospheric pressure). The compressed air is stored in a reservoir until needed.

The air service unit (FRL unit)

Before reaching the rest of the circuit, air passes through a Filter–Regulator–Lubricator (FRL) unit. The filter removes water and particles. The regulator sets a steady working pressure. The lubricator adds a fine oil mist to reduce wear on moving parts.

Directional control valves (DCVs)

Directional control valves direct air to different parts of the circuit. They are described by the number of ports (connections) and the number of positions (states). A 3/2 valve has 3 ports and 2 positions; a 5/2 valve has 5 ports and 2 positions.

Reading valve notation: The first number is the ports, the second is the positions. A 5/2 valve is the most common valve for controlling a double acting cylinder.

Actuators — cylinders

Single acting cylinder (SAC): Air pressure extends (pushes out) the piston rod. A spring returns the rod to its original position when pressure is released. SACs are used where a force is only needed in one direction.

Double acting cylinder (DAC): Air pressure is used for both extension and retraction. There are two air ports — one on each side of the piston. DACs provide force in both directions and are more versatile than SACs.

Flow control valves (restrictors)

Flow control valves limit the flow rate of air, controlling the speed of a cylinder. A one-way flow control valve (restrictor with check valve) allows free flow in one direction but restricts flow in the other, so extension and retraction speeds can be set independently.

Shuttle valve (OR valve)

A shuttle valve has two inlets and one outlet. If pressure is applied at either inlet, the ball inside moves to block the other inlet and allows air through to the outlet. This is a logical OR function — air passes through if input A or input B is pressurised.

T-piece (air supply splitter)

A T-piece simply splits one air supply into two branches, allowing the same supply to feed two different parts of a circuit simultaneously.

Section 3: Single Acting Cylinder Circuit

The simplest pneumatic circuit uses a single acting cylinder controlled by a 3/2 push-button valve. When the button is pressed, air flows to extend the cylinder. When released, the spring in the valve exhausts the air and the cylinder's own spring retracts the rod.

Worked Example — How the SAC circuit operates
1
At rest: The 3/2 valve is spring-returned to its normal position. Port 1 (supply) is blocked; port 2 (to cylinder) is connected to port 3 (exhaust). The cylinder spring holds the rod retracted.
2
Button pressed: The valve switches. Port 1 connects to port 2, supplying air to the cylinder. The piston extends against the spring.
3
Button released: The valve spring returns it to rest. Port 2 connects back to port 3. Air exhausts from the cylinder and the spring retracts the rod.

Section 4: Double Acting Cylinder Circuit

A double acting cylinder (DAC) requires air to be directed to either side of the piston. This is controlled by a 5/2 directional control valve, which routes air to the extend port or the retract port depending on its position.

The 5/2 valve has 5 ports: port 1 (supply), port 2 (to extend side), port 3 (exhaust from extend), port 4 (to retract side), and port 5 (exhaust from retract). When the valve switches, air to the cylinder reverses direction, actively driving the rod both ways.

DAC advantage over SAC: Because air actively retracts the DAC piston rod, it can exert a pushing force in both directions. The retraction force is slightly less than extension force because the rod reduces the effective piston area on the retract side.

Section 5: T-Piece — Splitting the Air Supply

A T-piece connector allows one compressed air supply to feed two or more separate branches of a circuit simultaneously. Both branches receive the same supply pressure. T-pieces are used when a single air source needs to power multiple actuators or valves at the same time.

Section 6: Shuttle Valve (OR Valve)

A shuttle valve (also called an OR valve) allows a cylinder to be operated from two separate control valves — either valve can extend the cylinder. This is useful when a machine needs to be operated from two different locations.

Inside the shuttle valve is a small ball. When air enters from one inlet, it pushes the ball to seal the other inlet and passes through to the outlet. If air enters the other inlet instead, the ball moves across and the same thing happens.

Logic function: A shuttle valve performs a logical OR operation. The cylinder extends if valve A or valve B (or both) is operated. This is useful for two-handed operation or remote control of a machine.

Section 7: Speed Control — Flow Control Valves

The speed of a pneumatic cylinder can be controlled by limiting the flow rate of air into or out of it. A flow control valve (restrictor) contains a variable needle valve in parallel with a check (non-return) valve.

Meter-in: The restrictor is placed on the supply side. It limits air flowing into the cylinder, slowing the stroke in that direction.

Meter-out: The restrictor is placed on the exhaust side. It limits air flowing out of the cylinder. This gives smoother speed control and is preferred in most applications because back-pressure supports the load.

One-way flow control valve: The check valve allows free flow in one direction and restricted flow in the other. This means you can control the speed of extension independently from retraction.

Section 8: Semi-Automatic Circuit

A semi-automatic circuit requires a manual input to start each cycle, but part of the operation happens automatically. A typical arrangement uses a DAC, a 5/2 main control valve, and a roller trip valve (limit valve) triggered by the cylinder reaching the end of its stroke.

The operator presses a start button to extend the DAC. At full extension, the rod trips the limit valve. This automatically pilots the 5/2 valve to reverse, retracting the cylinder. The operator must press the button again to start the next cycle.

Worked Example — Semi-automatic cycle
1
Start: Operator presses push-button → pilot signal switches the 5/2 valve → air extends the DAC.
2
End of stroke: Rod reaches full extension and operates the roller trip valve → pilot signal switches the 5/2 valve back → air retracts the DAC.
3
Wait: Cylinder is fully retracted. Circuit waits for the operator to press the button again.

Section 9: Fully Automatic Circuit

A fully automatic circuit repeats its cycle continuously without any manual input after the initial start signal. A second limit valve at the retracted position triggers the next extension automatically, so the cylinder cycles back and forth until a stop signal is given.

Fully automatic vs semi-automatic: In a semi-automatic circuit, one limit valve is used so the cylinder stops at the retracted position. In a fully automatic circuit, a second limit valve at the retracted end immediately triggers the next extension.

Section 10: Time Delay Circuit

A time delay valve introduces a controllable delay between an input signal and an output action. It consists of a flow control valve feeding a small reservoir, which builds up enough pressure over time to pilot a 3/2 valve.

By adjusting the flow control valve, the fill time of the reservoir — and therefore the length of the delay — can be changed. Time delays are used where a fixed wait is needed between operations, for example holding a part in a jig while adhesive sets before releasing it.

Worked Example — Time delay operation
1
Input signal received: Air flows through the adjustable restrictor, slowly filling the reservoir (capacitance vessel).
2
During delay: Reservoir pressure is below the pilot threshold — the output valve remains in its normal position.
3
Delay complete: Reservoir pressure reaches the pilot threshold — the 3/2 valve switches and the output signal is delivered to the actuator.
4
Reset: When the input signal is removed, the reservoir vents quickly through the check valve and the timer resets immediately.
